## 3. NEW STORY Global displacement crisis
_Other — importance 4.0 — 2 sources — latest reported 2026-05-12 11:33 UTC_

**Status:** New | **Confidence:** High | **Source agreement:** Broad | **Dispute:** None

**What changed today:** First detected today.

### Why it matters
The Internal Displacement Monitoring Centre’s new figures put conflict-driven internal displacement at a record high in 2025. NU.nl reports that more than 32 million people fled within their own countries because of conflict and violence last year. The Guardian gives the headline number as 32.3 million, up 60% from the previous year, and says that for the first time since data collection began in 2008, conflict-driven internal displacements exceeded disaster-driven ones. The total number of internally displaced people worldwide is reported at 82.2 million, while disaster-driven displacements reached 29.9 million. The significance is not just humanitarian scale but composition: violence is overtaking climate and natural hazards as the dominant immediate trigger. That matters for aid budgets, border politics and peace diplomacy. Internal displacement is often less visible than cross-border refugee flows, yet it can hollow out states from within and create long-term dependence on emergency systems.

### What to watch
- Which conflicts accounted for the largest share of the 2025 increase?
- Will donors shift funding from disaster response toward conflict displacement?
- Does the 60% jump reflect new wars, worsening existing wars or improved measurement?

## 3. NEW STORY Dutch flexwork reform
_Dutch Politics — importance 4.0 — 3 sources — latest reported 2026-05-12 16:06 UTC_

**Status:** New | **Confidence:** High | **Source agreement:** Broad | **Dispute:** None

**What changed today:** First detected today.

### Why it matters
The Dutch lower house approved a labour-market bill designed to push employers away from flexible work and toward permanent contracts. NOS reports that temporary-agency workers would gain the same employment conditions as regular staff, while zero-hours contracts would be restricted to pupils, students and state-pension-age workers. After three temporary contracts, an employer would have to wait three years before offering another temporary contract to the same worker. The social-affairs minister would also get more power to intervene when differences between agency workers and ordinary staff in a sector become too large, including through collective agreements. NU.nl says the end of the zero-hours contract is moving closer and notes the Netherlands has 2.7 million workers with flexible contracts. NOS adds that the Netherlands has the EU’s highest share of flex workers, including the self-employed. The Senate still has to consider the bill. The stakes are classic Dutch trade-offs: security for workers versus flexibility for employers in a tight labour market.

### What to watch
- Will the Senate pass the bill unchanged?
- How will employers redesign contracts if zero-hours work is curtailed?
- Will enforcement cover disguised flexibility through self-employment or agency structures?
